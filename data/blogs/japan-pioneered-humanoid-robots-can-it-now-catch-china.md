# Japan Built the First Humanoids — Then Watched China Outrun It Completely

There's something deeply uncomfortable about watching a conference you helped pioneer become a showcase for someone else's hardware. That's what happened at the Humanoids Summit in Tokyo this year. Of the roughly 40 robots on display, Chinese systems outnumbered Japanese ones by about three to one. One Japanese engineer called it "sad." I'd call it a reckoning.

## The Country That Started It All Is Now Playing Catch-Up

WABOT-1 rolled out of Waseda University in 1973. It walked slowly, gripped objects, and managed basic communication. That was the seed of everything Honda later poured into Asimo — a robot that could run, climb stairs, and wave at dignitaries for decades without ever selling a single unit. Asimo got retired in 2022, the same year ChatGPT appeared. Two years later, Unitree's G1 went on sale for sixteen thousand dollars. The math doesn't flatter Japan here.

What Japan still has is design expertise and institutional know-how. Hiroshi Ishiguro's Geminoid HI-6 — an android replica of its creator — made an appearance at the summit, chatting with visitors using a language model trained on Ishiguro's own writings. It's impressive technology, but it was one of very few Japanese humanoids present. Meanwhile, kids in Beijing were watching Booster Robotics' K1 bots dance to Michael Jackson. The price gap tells you everything: China's Mini Pi biped costs around three thousand five hundred dollars in its base form. Japan isn't even in that conversation yet.

## Why Did Japan Lose Ground?

Bad timing is part of it — humanoid development happened before anyone had figured out what to actually do with these machines domestically. But there's a deeper structural problem. Japan has a long history of building impressive technology that stays home while other countries commercialize it at scale. Think about those multifunction toilets Toto engineered decades ago; they only started gaining international traction recently, and even now they're mostly a sideshow to Japan's broader export story.

The industrial robot numbers tell an even starker tale. Japan dominated manufacturing robot density from 1994 to 2009, but by 2024 it had fallen to fifth place, behind South Korea (which hit 1,220 robots per 10,000 workers versus Japan's 446). China now operates roughly 2 million industrial robots — nearly 4.5 times more than Japan — and accounted for 54 percent of all global robot installations in 2024. Ani Kelkar at McKinsey put it plainly in an interview: "The recovery has not yet happened as Japan missed the rapid acceleration in AI for robotics and is now playing catch-up."

I could be wrong here, but I think the real issue isn't just technical lag — it's cultural reluctance to embrace messy scale. Japanese firms tend to refine until something is perfect before shipping it. Chinese firms ship first and iterate relentlessly. In a field where the biggest advantage right now is having mountains of real-world operational data, that difference is existential. You can't optimize what you haven't deployed. And you can't collect data if your product sits on a shelf collecting dust instead of doing useful work at Haneda airport or a factory floor in Shenzhen.

## What Japan Needs to Do (If It Still Wants To)

Tetsuya Ogata at Waseda is pushing for something that sounds simple but will be politically brutal: shared data infrastructure across the industry instead of every company hoarding its own silos. His AI Robot Association has collected around 80,000 hours of remote manipulation data — supposedly the largest dataset of its kind — and is building vision-language-action models with it. The idea is to create a precompetitive foundation that everyone builds on, then compete on applications on top of it. Ogata calls this a "third pole" alongside the U.S. and China. I respect the ambition, but I'll believe it when I see companies actually sharing data willingly. That's a hard pill for any industry to swallow.

McKinsey estimates a $100 billion opportunity in general-purpose robotics for Japan if it pivots hard enough. But "pivot" means deregulation, openness, and accepting that some of those elegant Japanese designs will need to become rougher, cheaper, and faster to market than they're comfortable with. It also means competing directly against machines that cost less than a decent car and are already being deployed in logistics trials across Asia.

## Key Takeaways

- Japan pioneered humanoid robotics decades ago but has ceded its lead as Chinese companies moved aggressively into mass production and cost reduction.
- The gap isn't just hardware — China dominates industrial robot deployment globally (54% of installations in 2024), and Japan's robot density has fallen from first to fifth place since 2009.
- Japan's path back involves shared data ecosystems and industry collaboration through groups like AIRoA, rather than competing on proprietary silos alone.
- The broader lesson mirrors Japan's mobile internet story: being first doesn't matter if you don't scale commercially and export effectively.

## What Comes Next

Japan's humanoid ambitions aren't dead — they're just delayed by an industry that's learned to move faster than it ever has before. Whether Tokyo can build a credible third option between Silicon Valley and Shenzhen depends less on clever engineering and more on whether its companies can stomach shipping imperfect things at scale. The old joke was that Japan made robots that looked great but couldn't do anything useful. Now China makes robots that can do things but might not look as polished. The question is which approach wins the next decade — and whether Japan can find a lane somewhere in between without losing its identity entirely.
