## America Just Declared War on Foreign Robots, But It Doesn't Know What It's Actually Fighting For

Here's what nobody's saying out loud about this new FCC ban: we've been sleepwalking into a two-tier robotics market for years, and most American companies were fine with it as long as they weren't the ones getting undercut on price. The FCC's Covered List expansion changes that calculus overnight, which is exactly why half the industry looks stunned right now.

## So What Did They Actually Do?

On July 28th, the FCC slapped foreign-produced mobile robots over 2 kilograms onto its national security blacklist. Any device with on-board sensing, wireless communication, and some degree of autonomy gets caught in the net — unless it weighs under 2 kg or whispers data at less than 200 kilobits per second. There goes any hope of a lightweight reconnaissance bot sneaking through the cracks without being declared a national security threat by committee. Power inverters used in solar panels got dragged in too, because apparently everything is now a vector for geopolitical risk if you manufacture it overseas with Chinese components somewhere in the chain. Existing certified devices are grandfathered in, so nobody has to rip anything off a shelf tomorrow. New products from abroad? Blocked at the border unless you can convince both DOD and the FCC you're not a threat — and even then you'll need an actual plan to build manufacturing in the U.S., not just slap an American logo on someone else's assembly line somewhere else.

The deadline for applications is January 2028. Which gives companies roughly three years to restructure entire supply chains or watch their products disappear from U.S. markets entirely.

## Who's Happy About This?

Brendan Schulman from Boston Dynamics called it "the first round in a series of policies that will define the success and growth of the industry for decades." That's either visionary or wildly optimistic, depending on whether you trust government policy to stay consistent across election cycles. Ghost Robotics' CEO Gavin Kenneally had stronger language about active spyware deployed inside U.S. infrastructure on Chinese-made machines — and predatory pricing that undercuts everyone else while subsidized by state-backed capital. Those are real concerns anyone who follows robotics knows about; I remember when Unitree opened up their SDK and suddenly researchers worldwide were poking at their systems looking for vulnerabilities. The exploit they found wasn't hypothetical — it was documented, public, and frankly embarrassing given how embedded those robots were becoming in research labs everywhere. So yeah, cybersecurity fears here aren't coming from nowhere.

But plenty of companies aren't panicking. Nic Radford at Persona basically shrugged and said his customers probably wouldn't have bought Chinese humanoids anyway because they want auditable tech with local support chains. Philipp Frey from ANYbotics told Spectrum their Swiss quadrupeds are already evaluated on reliability, cybersecurity certifications, and serviceability rather than raw cost per unit anyway. In other words, some companies just discovered they've been competing on quality this whole time while pretending they worried about cheap imports taking their lunch money. I could be wrong here, but that smells like survivor bias dressed up as strategic clarity. Companies that weren't losing ground don't get to claim the ban was designed to protect them specifically.

## Is a Blanket Ban Even Smart Policy?

Kyle Chan at Brookings Institution published his own assessment just weeks before this ruling and basically said Washington keeps treating these issues like they're isolated incidents instead of systemic challenges. He wants BIS centralized as a clearinghouse for decisions like this rather than letting them scatter across agencies with overlapping mandates — sounds reasonable until you realize centralization also means slower responses when threats actually do emerge piecemeal across different sectors. His bigger point was that crude import bans risk severing supply chain partnerships that aren't actually sensitive in ways that slow down innovation overall. Every time I hear an executive call for full decoupling from China I think about how Intel's attempt to decouple its chipmaking from TSMC ended up costing billions and years of schedule slippage before they admitted some dependencies aren't easily untangled without enormous pain. Robots are no different. A lot of sensor components, actuators, even specialized chips come through supply chains where tracing every component back to its origin is genuinely hard work — not because anyone's hiding anything necessarily, but because modern manufacturing is just incredibly layered by design. You're asking companies to audit systems of staggering complexity using regulatory frameworks built for simpler times. It reminds me of trying to fix legacy code by rewriting everything from scratch during production traffic. The impulse is understandable; the execution is brutal.

China's Commerce Ministry called it protectionism wearing a neutral mask, which is diplomatic but essentially accurate given how much DOD cited Unitree-specific incidents in justifying this move while publicly claiming country neutrality. That tension isn't subtle and everybody sees it clearly except apparently the people writing press releases about it.

## Key Takeaways

- The ban targets new foreign-manufactured mobile robots over 2 kg with onboard comms and autonomy, effective once finalized — existing certified gear stays legal.
- American firms competing on quality and support stand to gain market share if customers prioritize cybersecurity audits over rock-bottom pricing, though not all will pivot successfully.
- Blanket bans have historically failed to dislodge entrenched foreign competitors while raising costs for domestic buyers — see drones, EVs, and telecom equipment before this one.
- The January 2028 application deadline creates urgency but leaves little room for startups without existing manufacturing infrastructure or deep pockets for compliance costs.

## What Comes Next

Watch whether DOD actually starts approving applications or uses national security review as a slow gatekeeping mechanism indefinitely. If approvals trickle out selectively to allies' companies while Chinese-origin robots face permanent exclusion, we'll have our answer about what "country-neutral" really means in practice — which is whatever serves whoever controls the paperwork first.
