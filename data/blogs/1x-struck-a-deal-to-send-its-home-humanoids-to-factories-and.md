# They Built Home Robots Then Sent Them Into Factories Instead

You'd think designing a humanoid for the kitchen means designing one for the kitchen. But that's apparently not how 1X sees things anymore. The company behind the NEO Gamma has quietly pivoted toward factories and warehouses — leaving the cozy domestic pitch behind entirely. And honestly? I'm not sure that's their worst decision or their best one either. But the story behind *why* is worth unpacking.

## From Living Rooms to Loading Docks Overnight (Almost)

Here's what happened under the hood, roughly speaking. 1X launched with a compelling vision: build friendly humanoid robots that help people around their homes. Think carrying groceries upstairs, folding laundry, maybe handing your kid a juice box without knocking over anything fragile in the process. For years they talked up exactly this scenario at conferences and in demos that generated plenty of excitement but zero actual consumer sales data worth citing publicly. Then something shifted internally — likely driven by revenue pressure rather than pure engineering curiosity. Warehouses were suddenly calling. Companies running fulfillment centers needed automation desperately enough to consider bipedal machines seriously for basic pick-and-place tasks they'd previously handled with conveyor belts and human labor alone. So 1X redirected its NEO platform toward industrial contracts almost wholesale rather than continuing to wait for homeowners to open their wallets wide enough to justify production volume at scale. It's less of a dramatic reinvention and more of an overdue reality check dressed up as strategic evolution. Which isn't necessarily bad! Pragmatism keeps companies alive longer than dreams usually do. But it does mean anyone expecting their living room to fill up with helpful humanoids anytime soon should probably lower expectations considerably for now. Maybe indefinitely honestly.
