## Teradyne Is Finally Making Money Off Robots That Don't Suck (Much)

So Teradyne's robotics division just posted a 33% year-over-year revenue jump in Q2 2026. That's not a rounding error or a fluke — that's a company that actually figured something out. And the big drivers? U.S. demand and AI integration. Two things that should've been obvious five years ago but somehow weren't.

I remember when everyone in the industry was obsessing over humanoid robots and flashy demos at trade shows. Meanwhile, the actual money was sitting on factory floors in the form of collaborative robots — cobots — that bolt onto existing assembly lines and do the dull, repetitive stuff humans don't want to do anymore. Universal Robots, which Teradyne acquired back in 2015, basically invented this category. And it took the company roughly a decade to stop bleeding cash on the broader robotics side and start harvesting what that early bet actually produced.

## The AI Angle Is Real This Time

Here's where it gets interesting. Teradyne's been layering AI into its fleet of autonomous mobile robots — the MiR line — and into the software that runs its cobots. The difference this time versus past hype cycles is measurable. Revenue doesn't lie. When you see a 33% bump tied specifically to AI integration, it means customers are actually deploying these systems at scale, not just signing pilot programs to tick ESG boxes.

Think of it like upgrading from a basic GPS navigation app to one that learns your commute and reroutes you before you even notice there's a problem. That's what Teradyne's doing with its fleet management software — predictive routing, obstacle avoidance that adapts in real time, integration with warehouse management systems that actually talk to each other. It's not magic. It's just software getting good enough to make hardware investments pay off faster. But getting from "dumb robot" to "smart robot" required solving problems nobody talked about publicly for years: calibration drift, sensor degradation in dusty environments, and the fact that most factory floors look like a toddler's bedroom after a cereal explosion.

## Why U.S. Demand Spiked Now

The domestic growth story makes sense when you look at the broader picture. American manufacturers have been on-edge about supply chain fragility for years now. COVID showed everyone what happens when your entire production line depends on a single port in Shenzhen. Reshoring isn't just a political talking point anymore — it's a genuine operational strategy, and reshoring needs automation because you can't just magically clone the workforce you lost during the great resignation wave of 2021-2022.

Teradyne's positioned itself right in that gap. Their cobots are cheaper than traditional industrial arms and easier to program than a Swiss CNC machine. A factory worker can teach a UR robot a new task by physically guiding its arm through the motion — no coding degree required. That accessibility matters enormously when you're deploying robots across small and mid-sized shops that don't have dedicated engineering teams.

I could be wrong here, but I think this is the quarter where Teradyne crossed from "promising robotics company" to "actual robotics company" in terms of market perception. The competitors — FANUC, ABB, KUKA — have been sitting on their hands or moving too slowly with AI features. They've got brand recognition and installed bases, sure, but brand recognition doesn't pay your bills when customers are actively shopping for software-first solutions. Still, I'd watch those incumbents closely. They have deep pockets and they're not done fighting.

## Key Takeaways

- **U.S. manufacturing demand is the real engine** — reshoring trends and labor shortages are pushing adoption faster than expected.
- **AI integration is driving revenue directly**, not just as a marketing feature but as a tangible deployment differentiator.
- **Teradyne's early bet on Universal Robots is paying off** after years of building the ecosystem and customer trust.
- **Competitors are lagging on software sophistication**, which creates a window of opportunity that Teradyne is exploiting right now.

## What Comes Next

The question isn't whether this growth continues — it almost certainly will for at least another couple quarters given the tailwinds. The question is whether Teradyne can keep its software moat wide enough that the big players can't just buy their way in or build catch-up features fast enough. I'd put money on them holding the lead for now, but the robotics industry has a habit of surprising people who think they've figured it out.
